SLIDE 1 - Beyond Chatbots
0:00-0:45

Good afternoon. I am Robert McMurrer, the founder of Kira Labs in Newark, New Jersey. I am building Kira World and researching NewBrain, alongside local creative and authoring tools.

My question is practical: if an AI remembers something next week, how can I tell whose memory it is, where it came from, and whether it is still correct? If it says it saved a project or finished a task, how can I check that the action happened?

Today I will share engineering patterns and results we can support. A small language learner trained from scratch retained its earlier lessons with review, and we repeated that result across five new random seeds. A separate visual learner failed to generalize. I will explain both findings and their limits, alongside the earlier circuit measurements.

SLIDE 2 - Continuity across sessions
0:45-2:00

A conversation can look convincing while concealing a problem that appears later. A system may remember an outdated fact, mix two people's histories, or restart a job that already completed.

For Kira World, continuity means more than remembering a name. The intended system has distinct synthetic identities whose experiences and histories stay separate over time. That creates questions about correction, access, recovery and choices.

Imagine two invented residents. Kira saw a blue cup in a room. Lisa never visited that room. A later question should not give Lisa an autobiographical memory of seeing it. She might receive a shared fact if someone tells her, but that has a different source.

This is why I test boundaries around the conversation. What survives closing and reopening? What happens after a correction? Which records can an identity access? We need answers based on saved state and behavior.

SLIDE 3 - Local model + retained state
2:00-3:15

A language model has learned parameters. A conversation provides temporary context. An application may retain records in files or a database. These layers have different lifetimes and failure modes.

Our long-term goal is a synthetic person who develops capabilities and retains individual experiences. The present software has not established subjective experience, personhood or equivalence to a human mind.

Kira World currently uses its existing language backend. NewBrain now has a separate small trained command learner, but that extension has no across-turn memory or open conversation and does not replace the backend.

The architecture separates a reusable engine from each identity's records and learned state. That lets us evaluate a component without silently changing someone's history.

SLIDE 4 - Kira World: independent histories
3:15-4:30

Kira World is the larger setting for this work. The intended platform brings persistent synthetic identities into digital environments, with individual histories, choices and interaction. I describe the principle as connection without ownership.

A common engine does not imply a common autobiographical record. Two identities may use the same code or sensory machinery while retaining different experiences. Shared world facts also need their own representation. Knowing that a room exists differs from personally entering it.

I am presenting the architecture goal, not a finished platform or completed VR system. A portable review/runtime lane and World Shell exist. World Builder authoring remains paused. Avatar Builder is still developing bodies and rigs, and complete live identity and embodiment integration remains unfinished.

The useful design question is what each identity may read, write and report, including through retrieval, export and recovery.

SLIDE 5 - Identity isolation test
4:30-5:45

This is a teaching example with invented identifiers and one observation belonging to the first identity. The first can retrieve its observation. The second cannot retrieve it as its own memory.

We should test less obvious changes too. If we rename an identity, does the record still belong to its stable identifier? If we reopen the application, does the boundary hold? Sharing a room should not automatically create identical experiences.

Lisa can receive the statement that Kira saw a cup, with a source saying Kira reported it. That does not mean Lisa saw the cup herself.

This example illustrates a contract to test. Passing a small local exercise validates that exercise. It does not establish that every retrieval route in a larger application preserves identity separation.

SLIDE 6 - Corrections with history
5:45-7:00

Suppose a record says the cup was blue. Later we discover it was green. A correction should change the effective answer while retaining enough history to understand why the earlier answer differed.

In the teaching pattern, the correction has its own identifier, a reference to the earlier assertion and a source. The current view selects the corrected assertion. An audit view can still show the earlier one as superseded, where retention is appropriate.

Correction is different from deletion. A privacy deletion request can require removing information rather than keeping it indefinitely. A history-preservation rule should not override every retention choice.

Tests should check the current answer, the correction link and the intended treatment of the old record. Then repeat the checks after reopening and interrupted writes. A reassuring reply saying 'I updated my memory' is only one part of the evidence.

SLIDE 7 - Sources and hypotheses
7:00-8:15

A memory system needs to know how a record originated. An observed event, a person's report, an inferred explanation and an imagined event are different information.

If we add a dream or replay experiment, its output must retain its origin. A synthetic dream about visiting Mars must not become a claim that someone physically visited Mars. A hypothesis can inspire a test, but needs independent evidence before becoming a supported conclusion.

The same rule applies to an engineering assistant. An AI-generated component illustration does not establish that a supplier sells it. A plausible explanation is not a verified bill of materials. A paper's title alone does not establish the strength of its evidence.

The reusable lesson is to preserve the source, the assertion's scope and the correction history in a form the application can enforce.

SLIDE 8 - Failure, choice and held status
8:15-9:30

Persistent systems need a vocabulary for work that has not completed. Requested, held, running, completed, failed and uncertain should describe different conditions.

Video Studio is a useful example. When a request does not meet the applicable RAM and GPU requirements, it may remain saved in chat without an admitted render. That is a saved request and a held operation, not a finished movie. A new source proposal does not change an old launch gate by itself.

A system failure or unavailable capability should not become a story that an identity chose to refuse. We need a record of what happened.

My practical test compares the displayed status with operation and output records. If they disagree, I preserve the discrepancy. I do not replace a failed plan silently or declare success from a confident sentence.

SLIDE 9 - Lost acknowledgment and receipts
9:30-10:45

Consider a save or export that finishes, followed by a lost response. The client does not know whether it completed. Retrying as a new operation can create a duplicate.

The teaching pattern assigns an operation identifier before dispatch and retains its state. On reconnect, the client looks up that identifier and reconciles the receipt. An uncertain result stays uncertain until there is evidence. A missing acknowledgment does not establish either success or failure.

This pattern appears in Studio work as well as memory research. We review intended source and saved outputs separately, and preserve consumed attempts so recovery does not run them again by accident.

A receipt must correspond to the exact operation and artifact. Process cleanup needs its own evidence. The interface, durable record and actual result should tell the same story.

SLIDE 10 - A protocol for memory tests
10:45-12:00

For NewBrain, I want an experiment that can tell us whether a mechanism helps, rather than an example that merely looks impressive.

We define the task, observations, allowed state and training experience before comparing systems. We include strong simple controls, freeze learning rules and success thresholds before exposing evaluation cases, and distinguish a new holdout from an inspected example.

Ablations ask which part produced a change. For a delayed cue, we could compare persistent state with resetting state at each decision, or recurrence with recurrence removed. A plastic internal-connection candidate needs its own bounded implementation and review.

We record behavior and cost under the same conditions and keep failures and ties. A source review is not an executed benchmark. Mechanics checks, resource measurements and learning comparisons answer different questions.

SLIDE 11 - Review retention across five seeds
12:00-14:00

The standalone learner began with random weights and trained on a small command language. It has 25 vocabulary entries and 4,619 trainable parameters. No Qwen inference supplied its answers. Twenty engineering checks passed in the original run, including gradients and saving and reloading learned weights.

The first run kept old and new lessons at 100% with review, while old accuracy without review fell to about 40%. We preserved that result and repeated the same frozen, already exposed task using five fresh random seeds. This tests repeatability on the task, rather than a new general conversation benchmark.

Every review run retained all 216 old examples and answered all 162 new examples correctly. Without review, old accuracy ranged from 78/216 to 105/216, or 36.11% to 48.61%. The pooled old score without review was 458/1,080, or 42.41%. One no-review run also missed five of 54 new wording examples.

Both forks had 400 updates with batch size 24. Review used 7,200 new and 2,400 old examples, while no review used 9,600 new examples. A handwritten grammar also scored 100%. In-memory serialization and reloading checked all fifteen learned states in the replication, but it saved no new checkpoints to disk. Full conversation and persistent conversation memory remain unfinished.

SLIDE 12 - The visual learner failed to generalize
14:00-15:40

The separate visual learner received real synthetic pixels, not answer labels. It learned to name the color and shape in 32 by 32 RGB images. All ten engineering checks passed.

It answered all 96 training pictures correctly. When position, size and brightness changed, it scored 27/72, or 37.5%. It scored 0/48 on new color and shape combinations and 0/36 when both kinds of change occurred.

Color answers were correct throughout, while shape generalization failed. A capable pixel rule answered all 252 images correctly. At the fixed 0.70 confidence threshold, the learner still accepted 67 wrong answers across the three withheld splits: 22 after nuisance changes, 23 on new combinations and 22 when both changed. Confidence was not calibrated.

This one-seed result points to the teaching method and representation as the next problems. More units or RAM have not been shown to fix them. The visual weights were process-local and not saved, and this is not integrated perception. The earlier twelve Vision Q1 development cases are a separate package and remain unrun.

SLIDE 13 - Language cost and comparison
15:40-17:30

The scratch learner's 4,643 parameter entries, including 4,619 trainable entries, occupy 37,144 bytes of arrays. That is a parameter size, not total application RAM.

The training Python process reached a native peak of 40.30 MiB, including the interpreter, NumPy, data, several retained model copies and measurement code. The known process family reached 130.11 MiB at observed boundaries. These are short descriptive measurements, not continuous whole-machine enforcement.

Training and scoring took 0.837 seconds. Inside an already loaded process, the mean of 16 single-command calls was about 0.105 milliseconds. This measures the tiny command learner, not a conversational turn.

The review model and Qwen each scored 16/16 on the same narrow convenience subset, which only used red cubes. Qwen's 15 resident local API calls averaged 182.3 milliseconds, with protocol overhead. Different timing boundaries and Qwen's extensive pretraining prevent a fair whole-model speed ratio or a general equality claim. The circuit scaling result has its own scope on slide 24.

The five-seed replication's observed known-family native peak was 128.69 MiB, including the interpreter, shell and observed console host. That family total is different from the original Python-only 40.30 MiB peak and cannot be treated as isolated model RAM.

SLIDE 15 - Local data and identity boundaries
18:50-20:05

Local operation gives us a place to control storage and inspect execution. It does not automatically guarantee privacy or security.

The Kira World goal is separate records and access for each identity, with explicit sharing. I use invented records in teaching examples. Private autobiographical material does not belong in a public slide deck or judge packet merely because it exists on the same computer.

Network services, logs, exports and backups need scrutiny. Credentials and execution authority are different from research results. A private repository is different from a public artifact.

Tests should cover each route. What can be exported? What does a restored project include? What happens when an identity is renamed? Conversational fluency cannot prove those boundaries.

SLIDE 16 - Developmental learning experiments
20:05-21:20

We plan to teach capabilities gradually: hearing, visual concepts, grounded words, continuing memory and then communication. Human development motivates that order, but the proposed exercises do not establish the same biological mechanism.

The next tests use varied synthetic sounds and scenes, delay and distractors, sourced corrections, reopening and review. Rules and evaluation cases freeze before testing. New visual examples must prevent color or location from becoming a shortcut for shape.

The owner's test milestone is a full conversation that remembers after closing and reopening. The present command demonstration does not meet that milestone. Five-seed replication now provides repeatability evidence on the same exposed task. A persistent teaching journal remains pending here.

Avatar Builder proceeds alongside NewBrain. When anatomy, mesh, rig, movement and sensory feedback qualify, we can compare the same brain and common inputs in simple and detailed bodies. Then we can test richer feedback separately. Conversation tests can continue before the full body is ready.
